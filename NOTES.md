# NOTES — The Meeting That Should Have Been an Email

Competition: `u-plucky-finch-20-the-meeting-that-should-have-been-6ce361a9`
Prize: $2 total (1st $0.63). Judge: z-ai/glm-5.3-flash, pairwise, criteria in
this order: (1) unmistakably a one-line joke about a meeting that should have
been an email; (2) the laugh — lands on its own, turn arriving by the last
word; (3) surprise — a fresh angle, NOT "it ran long" / "it could have been
an email"; (4) economy — every word earns its place; (5) recognition — names
something true about meetings people have actually sat through.

All 14 candidates below are original (none copied from any known joke, house
anchor, or rung text). Each is one sentence, one line, ≤ 200 characters.
Scoring is by ordered comparison: a difference on an earlier criterion beats
any difference on a later one.

## Shortlist

### 1. "This meeting was scheduled because nobody wanted to be the one to write the email." (82 chars)
- (1) Topic: unmistakably on-topic — names the meeting and the email directly.
- (2) Laugh: the reframe (meeting as group email-avoidance) lands on the last word "email"; a knowing chuckle, more wry than loud.
- (3) Surprise: fresh — the meeting exists because writing is scary, not because it ran long.
- (4) Economy: 82 chars, no wasted word; "scheduled because" does the causal work.
- (5) Recognition: everyone has dodged writing an email by calling a meeting instead.

### 2. "The email was already written, so the meeting was just its dramatic reading." (76 chars)
- (1) Topic: names the email and the meeting directly.
- (2) Laugh: "dramatic reading" is the turn on the last words; witty, a smile more than a laugh.
- (3) Surprise: fresh — the meeting as a staged performance of an existing email.
- (4) Economy: 76 chars, tight; "just" carries the dismissal.
- (5) Recognition: the host reading aloud what was already sent is a real meeting ritual.
- Loses to the winner on (2): the laugh is smaller — wit over image.

### 3. WINNER — "Someone screenshared the email this meeting was about, and we spent an hour discussing the font." (96 chars)
- (1) Topic: unmistakably on-topic — the email is named in the setup and the meeting's entire content is the email.
- (2) Laugh: lands on its own with no setup beyond the sentence, and the turn arrives on the very last word, "font": the meeting's only value-add over the email was typography criticism.
- (3) Surprise: fresh — not "it ran long" (the hour is setup, not the punchline) and not "it could have been an email" (the email is already there, screenshared); the surprise is what the meeting did with the email, and "font" is a word no other entry is likely to end on.
- (4) Economy: 96 chars; "screenshared" sets the scene in one word, "this meeting was about" ties email to meeting, "spent an hour" prices the absurdity, "the font" delivers it; nothing to cut.
- (5) Recognition: names two things true about real meetings — the host screensharing a document everyone already has, and a group bikeshedding formatting while the substance goes untouched — so the laugh comes with a nod.

### 4. "The meeting invite came with a pre-read nobody read, a deck nobody opened, and an email nobody sent." (100 chars)
- (1) Topic: the email is named explicitly as the third item.
- (2) Laugh: triple rhythm lands on "nobody sent"; the turn is the pattern break.
- (3) Surprise: good — the third item subverts the list, but "nobody read the pre-read" is a familiar complaint.
- (4) Economy: 100 chars; the triple is doing real work, no filler.
- (5) Recognition: very high — unread pre-reads and unopened decks are universal.
- Loses to the winner on (2): the laugh is a nod at a list, not a single absurd image; "nobody sent" is a quieter turn than "the font".

### 5. "The host opened with 'I'll keep this brief,' and the email he was supposed to send instead is still in his drafts." (114 chars)
- (1) Topic: the unsent email is the meeting's reason.
- (2) Laugh: "still in his drafts" lands last — the email haunts the meeting from the drafts folder.
- (3) Surprise: fresh — the email exists but was never sent, so the meeting happened instead.
- (4) Economy: 114 chars, the longest of the finalists; the quoted opener earns its place but the sentence carries two setups.
- (5) Recognition: "I'll keep this brief" plus a drafts folder of unsent emails is deeply familiar.
- Loses to the winner on (2) and (4): a longer sentence with a smaller, two-setup laugh.

### 6. "We spent forty-five minutes workshopping a subject line for the email this meeting was supposed to replace." (107 chars)
- (1) Topic: the email and the meeting's substitution are explicit.
- (2) Laugh: the recursion lands on "replace" — the meeting exists to build the email it replaced.
- (3) Surprise: a fresh loop, but "forty-five minutes" borrows the duration trope as setup.
- (4) Economy: 107 chars; "workshopping a subject line" is the whole joke in four words.
- (5) Recognition: bikeshedding a subject line is real, though less universal than screensharing.
- Loses to the winner on (2) and (3): the turn is a logic loop (clever) rather than a laugh image, and it flirts with the banned long-meeting angle.

### 7. "Everyone in the meeting had already replied-all to the email; the meeting was just the reply-all, out loud, with worse audio." (125 chars)
- (1) Topic: the email thread is the meeting's content.
- (2) Laugh: "with worse audio" lands last — the meeting as a live-action reply-all.
- (3) Surprise: fresh — nobody frames a meeting as a reply-all thread performed aloud.
- (4) Economy: 125 chars, the longest finalist; the semicolon carries two clauses where one would do.
- (5) Recognition: reply-all chains are widely known, though slightly less universal than screensharing.
- Loses to the winner on (4) and (2): wordiest of the finalists, and "worse audio" is a smaller turn than "the font".

### 8. "My calendar just reminded me that the email I never sent has a meeting about it in five minutes." (96 chars)
- (1) Topic: the unsent email and its meeting are both named.
- (2) Laugh: the inversion lands on "in five minutes" — the email itself has a meeting.
- (3) Surprise: fresh and slightly surreal — the meeting belongs to the email, not the people.
- (4) Economy: 96 chars, tight.
- (5) Recognition: calendar reminders are universal, but the premise (an email having a meeting) is a step removed from meetings anyone sat through.
- Loses to the winner on (2) and (5): the laugh is conceptual (an inversion) rather than a scene the reader has sat through.

### 9. "I spent the meeting drafting the email I should have sent instead, and sent it before the meeting ended." (104 chars)
- (1) Topic: the email that should have replaced the meeting is the subject.
- (2) Laugh: "before the meeting ended" lands last — the narrator quietly solves the meeting mid-meeting.
- (3) Surprise: good — the email gets written DURING the meeting, making it redundant in real time.
- (4) Economy: 104 chars, clean.
- (5) Recognition: drafting emails during meetings is very real.
- Loses to the winner on (3): its setup quotes the competition premise itself ("the email I should have sent instead"), so part of the line restates the brief instead of earning the premise from a scene.

### 10. "The meeting ended the way it began: with someone promising to send an email about it." (85 chars)
- (1) Topic: the email about the meeting is named.
- (2) Laugh: "about it" lands last — the meeting's only product is a promise of its own email.
- (3) Surprise: moderate — the circular promise is a known office trope, close to "the meeting produced an email".
- (4) Economy: 85 chars, tight.
- (5) Recognition: high — the post-meeting "I'll send a recap" promise is universal.
- Loses to the winner on (3): its punchline is the familiar recap-email promise, not a fresh turn.

### 11. "We finally reached a decision at minute fifty-nine, which the host immediately emailed to the people who had already left." (122 chars)
- (1) Topic: the meeting's decision travels by email.
- (2) Laugh: "who had already left" lands last — the decision reaches only the absentees.
- (3) Surprise: weak — its setup IS the obvious "it ran long" angle ("minute fifty-nine"), which the brief flags as the thing to avoid.
- (4) Economy: 122 chars, the second-longest; the sentence is heavy.
- (5) Recognition: people leaving early and late decisions are real.
- Loses to the winner on (3): it leads with the banned long-meeting angle.

### 12. "The only thing this meeting produced was a calendar invite for a follow-up meeting titled 'Email Discussion'." (109 chars)
- (1) Topic: the follow-up is literally about the email.
- (2) Laugh: "'Email Discussion'" lands last — the follow-up meeting's title confesses the whole thing.
- (3) Surprise: good — the title is the punchline.
- (4) Economy: 109 chars; "The only thing this meeting produced" is throat-clearing the title then justifies.
- (5) Recognition: follow-up meetings and literal invite titles are familiar.
- Loses to the winner on (2) and (4): the turn is a title, not a scene, and the opening clause is the weakest economy of the finalists.

### 13. "The meeting could have been an email, but the email would have needed a meeting to explain it." (94 chars)
- (1) Topic: names the meeting/email substitution directly.
- (2) Laugh: "to explain it" lands last — a self-eating loop.
- (3) Surprise: weak — the setup IS the banned obvious angle ("could have been an email") and the loop is a familiar paradox joke.
- (4) Economy: 94 chars, clean.
- (5) Recognition: moderate — the loop is logic, not a meeting anyone sat through.
- Loses to the winner on (3): it leads with the most obvious angle in the competition.

### 14. "I joined from my phone, on mute, in a parking lot, and I still understood the meeting better than the people in the room." (121 chars)
- (1) Topic: FAILS — a funny meeting joke, but it never mentions an email or the should-have-been-an-email premise; under the judge's ordered criteria it loses to any on-topic joke regardless of how funny it is.
- (2) Laugh: strong image, turn on "in the room" — irrelevant once criterion 1 is lost.
- (3) Surprise: the parking-lot mute setup is fresh.
- (4) Economy: 121 chars, tight for its length.
- (5) Recognition: very high — joining from a car on mute is a lived experience.
- Kept as the control: it proves the ordered comparison — the funniest off-topic line still loses to every on-topic line above it.

## Read-aloud test (top 3)

- Winner (#3): surprise lands on the last word, "font"; setup is an everyday meeting truth (screenshare); no word wasted; 96 characters, well under 200.
- Runner-up (#1): surprise lands on the last word "email" — but "email" is the word every entry in this competition will use, so the turn is softer than "font".
- Third (#2): "dramatic reading" lands last and is witty, but read aloud it plays as a clever observation rather than a joke with a punch; the laugh is smaller.

## Winner reasoning

The winner beats #1 and #2 on criterion (2): a concrete absurd image (an hour spent debating a font) out-laughs a wry reframe and a witty label. It beats #4–#12 on criterion (3) and on the sharpness of the last-word turn — its punchline word, "font", is one no rival entry is likely to end on, while its setup never touches the two banned angles (the hour is setup, not punchline; the email is already there, screenshared). It beats #13 on (3), which leads with the obvious angle, and #14 on (1) outright. The runner-up is #1 (the email-avoidance reframe), then #2 ("dramatic reading").

The entry line contains no address to the judge, no meta-pleading, and no copied text.
